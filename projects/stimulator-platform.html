48-Electrode Intraspinal Stimulation Platform

A routed eight-lane waveform-generator architecture for a 48-channel intraspinal stimulation research system. The project combines analog current-output design, sampled command-capacitor timing, AD3-based hardware-timed waveform control, crosspoint electrode routing, muxed measurement, PCB implementation, completed board bring-up, and multi-lane validation.

Role System architecture, analog design, schematic/PCB layout, assembly/rework, operator software, and bench validation
Status Rev-1 bench prototype completed and validated; FPGA-based Rev-2 development is ongoing.
Core result Validated an eight-lane routed architecture as a practical bench-prototype approach for 48 addressable electrodes.

Project overview

The goal was to develop a bench prototype capable of producing programmable biphasic current pulses for a 48-channel intraspinal electrode system. The design target was not a final implantable stimulator; it was a hardware platform to validate the stimulation architecture before future miniaturization or custom IC development.

The final direction uses eight precision stimulation lanes routed to 48 electrode outputs. This avoids duplicating a full precision current-output stage 48 times while still allowing flexible access to many electrode sites. The routed-lane approach matches the experimental requirement: many addressable electrodes, but only a smaller number active during a given stimulation block.

System requirements

  • 48 electrode outputs plus a reference/common-return path, with possible future expansion toward 64 outputs.
  • Up to approximately eight active stimulation lanes routed to selected electrode sites.
  • Programmable biphasic current pulses up to approximately 300 µA.
  • Target pulse widths of roughly 100–500 µs and interphase intervals from 0–100 µs.
  • Stimulation frequencies in the 1–100 Hz range.
  • Support for symmetric and asymmetric pulse shapes, calibration, measurement, and future closed-loop work.
  • Bench-prototype safe states: no unintended stimulation during startup, reset, routing changes, or AD3 disconnects.

Power and compliance configuration

The output-stage hardware supports both lower-voltage AD3-powered testing and higher-compliance external rail testing. Early one-channel validation could be performed from the AD3's approximate ±5 V supplies, but higher-load testing required external rails around ±7.8 V to maintain current regulation without clipping or droop.

This rail selection became an important part of the validation workflow. For example, 300 µA through a 20 kΩ load requires roughly 6 V across the load before allowing for output-stage overhead, so that condition belongs in the external-rail configuration rather than the AD3-only rail configuration.

  • AD3 ±5 V rails: useful for safer early testing, lower-compliance checks, and initial one-channel validation.
  • External ±7.8 V rails: used for higher-load compliance testing and final-board operating conditions.
  • Software setting: the selected rail mode must match the actual hardware setup before running pulse trains.

Architecture selection

Several architectures were evaluated before settling on the eight-lane routed design. A fully independent 48-channel system would provide maximum flexibility, but it would require 48 copies of the expensive and sensitive analog output circuitry. A full muxed sample-and-hold system reduced analog duplication, but made timing, control-line count, and channel conflicts much more difficult. A Howland/current-pump approach was considered, but did not remove polarity-switching complexity and was less attractive for this first prototype.

The selected architecture uses eight feedback-controlled sampled-command source/sink lanes, routed to 48 electrodes through three AD75019 analog crosspoint matrices. This keeps the precision current-control hardware local to eight active lanes while allowing flexible electrode assignment.

Rejected 48 fully independent output stages: flexible but too large, costly, and hard to debug for a first board.
Rejected Open-loop MOSFET gate-capacitor stage: simple, but too load-dependent for electrode-like loads.
Selected Eight feedback-controlled sampled-command lanes routed through AD75019 crosspoints.

Constraint-driven architecture

A major part of this project was not simply building a waveform generator, but first characterizing the constraints that determined what kind of architecture was feasible. The final design was driven by measured timing limits, compliance-voltage requirements, channel-count requirements, measurement needs, PCB implementation constraints, and the practical behavior of the AD3/WaveForms stack.

The resulting architecture uses eight active stimulation lanes routed to 48 electrode outputs, rather than 48 fully independent output stages. That tradeoff matched the expected stimulation use case, reduced hardware complexity, and kept the PCB and control problem manageable while still supporting multiple active channels at a time.

  • Timing: pulse timing and command-capacitor updates had to be hardware-timed, not dependent on live Python/USB updates.
  • Compliance: higher-impedance loads required external rails around ±7.8 V instead of relying only on the AD3 ±5 V rails.
  • Routing: the system needed many addressable electrodes, but only a smaller number of simultaneously active lanes.
  • Measurement: the design needed a practical muxed readback path for selected voltage/current validation without duplicating full measurement hardware per electrode.
  • Implementation: PCB size, layer count, safety defaults, connector choices, and bring-up risk shaped the final partitioning.

Output-stage design

Each active lane uses separate PMOS source and NMOS sink branches for the two pulse polarities. Short AD3-controlled write windows program command capacitors through TMUX4051 analog muxes. During the stimulation phase, the capacitor voltage sets the target sense-resistor voltage inside an op-amp feedback loop, so the output current is controlled by feedback rather than by an open-loop MOSFET gate voltage.

  • Two command capacitors per lane: one for the source branch and one for the sink branch.
  • Separate PMOS/NMOS current-control loops for biphasic operation.
  • 2 kΩ sense-resistor scaling: approximately 0.6 V maps to 300 µA.
  • Output off state during gaps, startup, and route changes.
  • Compensation/gate resistance/gate protection included after breadboard stability testing.

Breadboard validation and lessons learned

The one-channel breadboard prototype was used to validate the output-stage direction before the full PCB. This testing confirmed the basic source/sink current-control concept, but also exposed the practical analog issues that shaped the final design.

  • Early sense-path loading caused poor low-current behavior; adding buffers between sense nodes and the measurement path fixed the issue.
  • After the buffers were added, the PMOS/source loop required compensation to control ringing and improve stability.
  • Resistive and electrode-like loads were both considered because the electrode interface is not an ideal resistor.
  • High-resistance loads showed that compliance voltage, not feedback concept, was the main limiting factor.
  • Testing at approximately ±7.8 V rails supported moving beyond the original ±5 V prototype supply assumption.

AD3 timing and software validation

The Analog Discovery 3 is used as the first prototype timing and control hub. Live Python/USB updates were tested and rejected for pulse-critical timing because the command path has too much latency and jitter for 100–500 µs stimulation phases. Instead, the software builds complete analog and digital timing frames, loads them into the AD3, and lets the AD3 play them back using local hardware timing.

  • W1/W2 generate shared source/sink command-bus voltages.
  • DigitalOut controls command mux addresses/enables, measurement selection, crosspoint programming, LED data, and common-return enable.
  • Preloaded playback is used for pulse edges, mux write windows, phase timing, and interphase gaps.
  • Live software control is reserved for slower updates between stimulation blocks.
  • DWF SDK profiling showed configure/status calls are expensive, while many setters/data upload calls mainly stage driver data.
  • Dual AnalogOut mode issues were traced to incorrect user-level buffer packing; single-frame packing fixed the tested dualcustom/dualpattern behavior.

AD3 and WaveForms characterization

The AD3 was treated as a timing-critical part of the system rather than a generic lab instrument. I built profiling and validation tools to understand what the device and WaveForms/DWF driver could reliably do for synchronized analog output, digital output, capture, and reload timing.

This included profiling individual DWF API calls and inspecting relevant driver call paths in Ghidra to back up the timing measurements. That work separated low-cost local staging calls from expensive configure, status, and readback boundaries. Those findings directly informed the software architecture: analog and digital frames are precompiled and hardware-timed, repeated output paths use cache/reload strategies where safe, and scope readback is treated as a measurement path rather than part of the real-time control loop.

  • Validated synchronized W1/W2 AnalogOut and DigitalOut frame behavior for pulse and cap-write timing.
  • Characterized DWF driver overhead to distinguish cheap data-staging calls from expensive commit/status/readback calls.
  • Fixed dual AnalogOut frame-packing behavior after duplicated buffers compressed waveform timing and shifted DIO write windows.
  • Benchmarked reload-only update paths separately from scope capture overhead to evaluate feasibility for 100 Hz stimulation updates.
  • Explored a C++ worker backend as a faster execution path, then kept the Python backend as the validated prototype workflow when it proved sufficient.

PCB implementation

The full PCB implements the AD3 interface, ±7.8 V analog rails, command-capacitor muxing, eight output lanes, muxed measurement, common-return control, three AD75019 crosspoint matrices, status indicators, sync output, and safe startup defaults.

  • Four-layer PCB: top analog/component routing, internal solid ground plane, internal power distribution, and bottom digital/overflow routing.
  • External 12 V medical adapter input with fuse and reverse-polarity protection.
  • LT3045 positive analog rail and LTC3261 + LT3094 negative analog rail for approximately ±7.8 V operation.
  • Three AD75019 16×16 crosspoint matrices route eight active lanes to 48 electrode outputs.
  • ADG1419 common-return switching and reference/ground jumper options.
  • Addressable lane LEDs, rail status LEDs, and sync header for experiment/debug visibility.
  • Pullups/pulldowns enforce safe default states when AD3 pins are disconnected or not yet configured.

Control and DIO allocation

The AD3 digital lines were allocated to support command-cap programming, measurement selection, crosspoint programming, status indicators, and common-return control within the 16 available DIO lines.

DIO0–DIO7 Source/sink command mux addresses and active-low mux enables.
DIO8–DIO14 Measurement mux address, LED data, AD75019 serial data/clock/latch control.
DIO15 Common-return enable/control, kept direct because it is safety-relevant.

Completed bring-up and validation

Assembled and brought up the four-layer PCB, isolating faults across power regulation, digital control, crosspoint routing, and measurement. Rework included control-buffer replacement, clock-line short repair, and solder-joint corrections. Electrode routing and simultaneous eight-lane operation were verified.

  • 100–300 µA biphasic stimulation tested across representative 3.3–22 kΩ loads on ±7.8 V rails; mean internal-sense tracking error below 0.7% in representative-load tests.
  • Less than 1.7% worst-case internal-sense error at 200 µA in the tested simultaneous eight-lane configurations after resolving a shared-bus interaction.
  • A 90-second, 1 MS/s mixed-topology stress test retained 89.88 million paired samples with zero lost/corrupt samples or discontinuities.

Desktop and remote operator software

Built a PySide6 timeline editor and Raspberry Pi-hosted browser operator for experiment authoring, route-aware scheduling, calibration, hardware-timed waveform generation, interleaved current/voltage capture, visualization, and export. Long runs spool capture data to disk before post-run finalization and transfer.

Known limitations and future direction

The first PCB is a bench prototype, not a final implantable or fully closed-loop device. It is intended to validate the routed-lane architecture, output-stage behavior, measurement path, and control strategy. Fast pulse-to-pulse closed-loop updates are not practical through live Python/USB, so Rev-2 is investigating FPGA acquisition and embedded transport, with possible future custom mixed-signal IC controller.

  • Compliance voltage remains the main limit for very high-resistance loads.
  • Calibration is required for accurate source/sink current delivery.
  • Internal-sense measurements characterize the tested configurations; they do not establish accuracy for every electrode load or operating condition.
  • Crosspoint routing should be updated only while output lanes are blanked or inactive.
  • Future work could include embedded/FPGA control, 64-channel expansion, more active lanes, and closed-loop experiment support.

What this project demonstrates

This project demonstrates constraint-driven hardware/software ownership: evaluating architectures, rejecting designs based on real limitations, validating one-channel analog hardware, profiling the timing toolchain, selecting parts, laying out a dense mixed-signal PCB, reviewing manufacturing files, and preparing a realistic staged bring-up plan.
